Abstract
Multispectral (MS) imaging reveals latent content in his-
torical documents by leveraging material-specific spectral
signatures. Low-rank decompositions such as Nonnegative
Matrix Factorization (NMF) effectively extract these com-
ponents, but selecting the appropriate rank remains an open
challenge in unsupervised settings. We propose PRISM,
a structured autoencoder that embeds a tri-factor NMF
into a convolutional architecture and integrates similarity-
driven pruning to automatically infer the effective number
of components during training. The encoder enforces spa-
tial attention to produce coherent abundance maps, while
the decoder reconstructs interpretable spectral signatures
through constrained linear decoding. This formulation
yields compact, interpretable representations with no su-
pervision or manual rank tuning. A Minimum Description
Length criterion estimates the optimal rank directly from the
input cube, balancing model complexity with data recon-
struction. Experiments on historical manuscripts and cross-
domain remote sensing datasets demonstrate PRISM’s abil-
ity to produce meaningful decompositions, with few compo-
nents and improved interpretability compared to fixed-rank
NMF and deep learning baselines.
1. Introduction
Model pruning techniques effectively reduce the complex-
ity of overparameterized deep networks, yet they often op-
erate with fixed compression ratios without revealing why
certain parameters are removed or offering principled stop-
ping criteria [3, 31]. Simultaneously, interpretability re-
mains a critical challenge, particularly in domains where
understanding the model’s internal representations is as im-
portant as its predictive accuracy. This is especially true for
Blind Source Separation (BSS) problems, where the goal is
to decompose a mixed signal into its underlying constituent
sources without prior knowledge of the sources or the mix-

in medical imaging, to isolate tissue types and anatomical

beforehand, a critical limitation in multispectral (MS) his-

is unknown (e.g., the number of distinct inks, substrates,
auxiliary markings, and degradation products present).

Even sophisticated modern segmentation approaches, in-
cluding Vision Transformers (ViT), often struggle with doc-
ument images. This is primarily due to the inherent fine-
grained nature of text, where thin strokes and delicate struc-
tures are difficult for patch-based processing. Even SoTA
segmentation transformers, presented as global and multi-
task, exhibit limited performance: Dinov2 [33] may help
identify document regions but struggles with character-level
segmentation, while Segment Anything Model (SAM) [23]
cannot find a balance between over-segmentation (split-
ting text into individual characters) and under-segmentation
(merging multiple characters into coarse blobs), as seen
on Fig. 1. These models also don’t benefit from the advan-
tages of MS imagery with a fixed number of channel design,
tailored for RGB input. An ideal unsupervised method for
multiband image decomposition should not only separate
the layers accurately but also be interpretable (i.e., reveal-
ing a clear spatial distribution for each component with its
associated spectral signature) and adaptive to diverse con-
figurations (e.g., variable depth, resolution or wavelengths).
To address these limitations, we introduce PRISM,
Pruning for Rank-adaptive Interpretable Segmentation
Model. This framework re-envisions pruning not only as
a compression tool, but as a mechanism for dynamic rank
selection within an NMF-inspired decomposition model.
PRISM employs a lightweight, asymmetric convolutional
autoencoder architecture. The encoder uses spatial attention
to learn structured spatial abundance maps from the multi-
band image input. The decoder enforces interpretability
through a nonnegative linear layer, reconstructing the im-
age from these abundances and learned spectral signatures.
Crucially, PRISM starts with an overcomplete set of poten-
tial components and integrates a learnable pruning mecha-
nism that progressively deactivates weights tied to the most
redundant maps during training. This process is guided by
orthogonality constraints encouraging distinct components
and by a criterion inspired by the Minimum Description
Length (MDL) principle [11, 43]. The latter balances re-
construction fidelity with model compression (linked to still
active map count) to identify the optimal rank. The convo-
lutional structure facilitates information redistribution from
pruned components to active ones, effectively merging se-
mantically related features. PRISM thus learns both the
decomposition and the optimal number of components di-
rectly from the data, yielding interpretable low-rank repre-
sentations, tailored for challenging document analysis tasks.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
i) We introduce a new pruning approach guided by a simi-
larity criterion with an overcomplete-to-optimal training
strategy, enhancing model interpretability and segmenta-
tion quality.
ii) To preserve the spatial relationship between pixels, we
implement a new lightweight hybrid NMF–convolutional
autoencoder structure capable of processing whole MS
and HS cubes of various resolutions and depths.
iii) Unlike existing models that integrate a two-factor NMF,
we propose a novel architecture with a tri-factorization
scheme and a non-local attention module for a more flex-
ible representation and better focus on important regions.
iv) We validate PRISM on real datasets, showing its effec-
tiveness for text binarization via MS cube decomposi-
tion. Quantitative and qualitative results confirm SoTA
performance for unsupervised historical document seg-
mentation. Generalization to remote sensing related task,
demonstrates method adaptability, key for handling the
unpredictable structure of historical documents.
2. Related work
PRISM integrates concepts from several research areas. We
position our work relative to recent advances in (i) blind de-
composition and spectral unmixing, (ii) models combining
deep learning with matrix factorization principles and (iii)
interpretable pruning and unsupervised rank selection.
Blind decomposition and spectral unmixing. Blind Im-
age Decomposition (BID) seeks to separate a composite im-
age into its constituent layers when both the sources and
the mixing process are unknown [13]. This general task
encompasses various applications, including removing en-
vironmental effects on images [44], decomposing images
and videos into dynamic foreground layers and static back-
grounds [1], or separating illumination effects for auto-
matic white balancing [46], with recent deep learning ap-
proaches significantly advancing the area. In remote sens-
ing and medical imaging, an analogous problem is spectral
unmixing, which decomposes hyperspectral (HS) or multi-
spectral (MS) images into a set of pure spectral signatures
(i.e., endmembers) and their corresponding spatial abun-
dance maps [22, 32]. In HSI analysis, transformer-based
architectures combined with diffusion models, like Diff-
Unmix [48], reformulate denoising and unmixing as condi-
tional abundance generation, leveraging multi-scale atten-
tion for improved endmember extraction. Other works pro-
pose novel paradigms like “unmixing before fusion” to gen-
erate synthetic HSI data by learning generative models in a
lower-dimensional abundance space [47]. While powerful,
these methods often rely on large pre-trained models, re-
quire task-specific heuristics, or assume a fixed number of
components. Furthermore, their application to the unique
challenges of historical document images (e.g., fine textual
details, bleeding or overlapping inks, few annotated data,
varying degradations, etc.) is not straightforward.

Hybrid Autoencoder–Factorization models. A growing
body of research explores the integration of classical matrix
factorization techniques, particularly NMF, with deep neu-
ral networks to leverage the strengths of both approaches.
These hybrid models often aim to enhance interpretabil-
ity by constraining the network’s latent space or weights
according to factorization principles. In the HS unmixing
field, Autoencoder (AE) networks were first introduced us-
ing a pixel-wise approach (i.e., input vector representing a
single data point) [27]. While this early work effectively
estimated abundance-like representations decoded via fully
connected layers, the explicit connection to matrix factor-
ization was not fully drawn. Subsequent research in re-
mote sensing built upon this, introducing deeper architec-
tures like variational AEs [45], recurrent structures such as
LSTMs [50], 3D CNN for spatial-spectral feature extrac-
tion [1], and more recently incorporating ViT with spatial-
spectral attention [39]. Concurrently, non-neural factor-
ization methods like NMF and Archetypal Analysis con-
tinue to be effectively applied to HS unmixing [49, 51]. A
key development in bridging unmixing AEs with convex
NMF was a series of work by Palsson et al., summarized
in [38], interpreting the AE’s decoder weights as endmem-
bers and the latent features as abundances. To address the
loss of spatial context inherent in pixel-wise approaches, a
lightweight convolutional AE structure was also introduced,
demonstrating strong performance, particularly in generat-
ing spatially coherent abundance maps [37]. In parallel,
for MS document image decomposition, advanced related
factorization concepts have proven their effectiveness, such
as NMF variants with optimization on orthogonal mani-
folds (MA-ONMF) [41] or variational Bayesian NMF [42],
which demonstrated interest in the task of model selection.
AE structures have also been explored for the text binariza-
tion task [4], but no hybrid structures have been observed.
Interpretable pruning and unsupervised rank selection.
Recent efforts in interpretable pruning leverage similarity
metrics to identify and remove redundant network compo-
nents like weights, filters, concepts, channels, or even layers
[2, 5, 28]. While this offers a degree of interpretability by
targeting similarity-based redundancy, such approaches, to
our knowledge, have not been directly formulated to per-
form dynamic rank selection for unsupervised decomposi-
tion tasks. While rank selection (i.e., determining optimal
number of components in images) remains an open chal-
lenge in computer vision, only very few recent approaches
have emerged, even for Vision Transformers (ViT), which
have become foundational for segmentation tasks. Despite
recent advances incorporating hierarchical clustering, meth-
ods such as Mask2Former [6] are designed for dataset-
specific applications, requiring extensive pre-training on la-
beled data. DINOv2 [33] proposes a self-supervised model,
extracting robust visual representations directly from data,
allowing SoTA performances on downstream tasks even
without fine-tuning. However, it relies on adding a trained
segmentation head, either a linear layer or a Mask2Former
head, that learns the mapping between the extracted features
and the predefined labeled materials. Segment Anything
Model (SAM) [23] employs a promptable ViT-based image
encoder for universal image segmentation. It also proposes
an image automatic segmentation with a predefined visual
prompt and a masks merging strategy based on quality met-
rics and non-maximum suppression. However, as discussed
earlier, the quality of the produced segmentation highly de-
pends on the input prompt, requiring user involvement for
alignment, and is not suitable for document images. In
medical imaging, where the scarcity of large-scale anno-
tated datasets is also a challenge, MS-Former [20] proposes
a transformer architecture with self-supervisory regulariza-
tion for unsupervised segmentation of skin and lung images.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the only ViT-based
method capable of dynamic rank selection with complete
unsupervised training for multiband image segmentation.
Relation to PRISM and key novelties. PRISM builds
upon these hybrid methodologies, specifically targeting
document image segmentation. It employs a convolutional
AE architecture, adapted from [37], combined with orthog-
onal NMF principles [41]. Crucially, PRISM distinguishes
itself from the aforementioned fixed-rank methods by intro-
ducing a dynamic pruning mechanism to determine the op-
timal number of underlying factors (r). Unlike MDL-based
approaches requiring post-hoc rank selection after multi-
ple NMF runs [43], or variational Bayesian NMF methods
that impose an automatic relevance determination (ARD)
prior on the optimization but require per-input hyperparam-
eter tuning [42], PRISM enables MDL-guided rank selec-
tion within a single end-to-end training process. While
MS-Former [20] offers a related online adaptation for med-
ical imaging, PRISM differs by maintaining both NMF
strengths and CNN high-resolution efficiency. By integrat-
ing these elements, PRISM aims to achieve a more inter-
pretable and adaptive decomposition of multiband images.
3. The proposed model
3.1. Problem formulation
In spectral data imaging, the Linear Mixing Model (LMM)
is a widely used framework to describe the generation of
MS/HS images. Under the LMM, each observed pixel in
the image is assumed to arise from a convex combination of
a finite set of spectral signatures (known as endmembers),
perturbed by additive noise. Let Y ∈ Rb×n
+ be the observed
spectral data matrix, where b is the number of spectral bands
and n the number of pixels. The LMM expresses the data
as :

3.2. Proposed PRISM model
The proposed autoencoder-like NMF model, PRISM, con-
sists of an encoder-decoder structure with a non-local at-
tention layer and an interpretable pruning mechanism de-
signed to automatically estimate the number of components
found in the input MS scene during training. The encoder
is designed to extract a rich feature representation from an
input MS cube, Y ∈ Rh×w×b
, where h × w is the spatial
size and b is the number of spectral bands. It generates the
abundance maps A ∈ Rh×w×r
, where each map represents
the relative proportions of spectral endmembers present at
each pixel. Here, the rank r is initialized to a large number,
with maps being progressively deactivated by the pruning
process. Following Eq. (3), the decoder combines the acti-
vated abundance with a learned scale matrix S. It then re-
constructs the input image with learned endmembers E. An
overview of the whole architecture is illustrated in Fig. 2.
Encoder. The encoder consists of four sequential layers
designed to process the entire set of MS bands simultane-
ously. In contrast to existing approaches [37] that adopt a
patch-based strategy, our encoder operates on the full im-
age, extracting useful relationships from all pixels. It be-
gins with a 2D convolutional layer employing c = 4 × b
kernels. The resulting feature maps are fed into an at-
tention block, which refines the feature representation by
emphasizing informative regions. The encoder concludes
with another depthwise 2D layer, followed by a pointwise
convolution, creating an expand-and-compress pattern that
captures spatial and channel-wise dependencies before out-
putting r maps. Finally, a temperature-scaled softmax func-
tion is used to handle the nonnegativity of the abundances,
elegantly satisfying both ANC and ASC conditions. By
adjusting the temperature hyperparameter, the sparsity of
abundance coefficients can be tailored to match the unmix-
ing requirements (i.e., mixed materials for remote sensing
unmixing or pure pixels for text segmentation).

Decoder. In contrast to models utilizing symmetric
encoder-decoder structures like [1], our PRISM architecture
incorporates a lightweight, nonnegative decoder that pre-
serves the interpretability of its learned components. This
decoder reconstructs the b input spectral bands from the
r abundance maps (A) by emulating the tri-factorization
Y ≈ ESA. Specifically, the r abundance maps are first
processed by an interaction matrix S, which is a learnable
r×r nonnegative matrix. The resulting r transformed maps
then pass through a final unbiased pointwise convolution,
whose b×r nonnegative weights constitute the learned end-
member matrix E and produce the b output bands. This
streamlined, asymmetric design significantly reduces com-
putational demands and the total parameter count; for in-
stance, the decoder’s learnable parameters for both S and
E totalled r(r + b) = 2088 for a 162-band hyperspectral
image, before pruning. Both E and S are constrained to be
nonnegative and are updated via backpropagation.
Attention block. The core of our attention block is adapted
from the original Large Kernel Attention (LKA) [12] that
is designed to enhance feature representation by capturing
both local and global relationships between pixels of MS
images. The module begins by processing the input feature
map through a standard 2D convolutional layer (DW-Conv)
with a 5 × 5 kernel size to extract local spatial features.
This is followed by a dilated depth-wise convolution (DW-
D-Conv) with a 7 × 7 kernel, which expands the receptive
field without increasing parameters, capturing broader con-
text by processing each channel independently. A pointwise
convolution then combines features across all channels, re-
fining the representation. The resulting feature map is mul-
tiplied element-wise with the original input feature map,
acting as an attention mechanism that emphasizes informa-
tive regions and suppresses less relevant features. Finally,
a pointwise convolution, with the same number of output
channels as the input, further refines the features, producing
the final output. Attention block is detailed in Supp. Mat.
Interpretable pruning. At the initial state, the model
is deliberately overcomplete, with more abundance maps
than objects, enabling the network to capture a comprehen-
sive set of all potential components, as done in [20, 42].
From these starting rinit abundance maps, an iterative train-
ing strategy is employed where the model alternates be-
tween training until early stopping, and a pruning step that
deletes the least informative map. At each pruning step, the
model is forced to adapt itself through merging, shifting,
or removal of elements; effectively distilling its knowledge
into a more compact representation. This strategy offers
the significant advantage of providing interpretability into
the model’s focus and enabling control over its decision-
making process. To identify the least informative map, a
pairwise similarity score between all unpruned maps is cal-
culated to identify the most similar pair. From this pair, the
map with the smallest Frobenius norm (i.e., the map repre-
senting the smallest image proportion) is added to the set
of pruned maps. Each map in this set sees the pointwise
convolution weights producing it being masked, as for its
corresponding decoder weights, which represent the asso-
ciated endmember. The similarity formula we developed is
adapted from the cross-correlation coefficient. To mitigate
an object fragmentation side effect, which may be induced
in early overcomplete steps, a spatial smoothing operation
is applied prior to calculation, using a Gaussian-like filter.
We observed that if two maps represented similar spatial re-
gions, having a small object within a larger one was often
more penalized than the same object split into two separated
equal parts. Therefore, the difference between the two maps
activation was included in the equation. Moreover, the spec-
tral angular distance between the corresponding endmem-
bers was incorporated to penalize materials having similar
endmembers but located at different spatial positions. The
final similarity formula, capturing both spatial and spectral
similarity between materials, is then expressed as follows:
  \mathcal {S}_{i,j} = \frac {1}{| {\langle \mathcal {A}_i - \mathcal {A}_j, \mathbf {1} \rangle } |+\epsilon }\frac { {\langle \mathcal {A}_i, \mathcal {A}_j \rangle }} {\| A_i \| \| A_j \|} + \lambda _{\text {SAD}'}\mathcal {L}_{\text {SAD}'}(E_i, E_j), \label {eq:sim}  (4)
where LSAD′ (Ei, Ej) is the spectral angular similarity be-
tween endmembers, λSAD′ is a parameter added to scale this
distance, ⟨·, ·⟩ is the inner product, and ϵ prevents division
by zero (see Supp. Mat. for ablation and hyperparameters).
Objective function. The loss function is composed of three
terms. The reconstruction error between the input MS cube
and the generated output is measured using a combination
of Spectral Angle Distance (SAD) and MSE. SAD, a scale-
invariant measure, addresses spectral variability by eval-
uating differences along the depth dimension [38], while
MSE enforces stricter material discrimination by encourag-
ing element-wise reconstruction. A third term, Lorth, en-
forces the orthogonality of the abundance matrix A, to pro-
mote spatially distinct and localized segments. The total
loss formula, detailed in Supp. Mat., is given as follows:
  \mathcal {L}_\text {total} = \mathcal {L}_\text {SAD} + \lambda _{1}\mathcal {L}_\text {MSE} + \lambda _{2}\mathcal {L}_\text {orth}. \label {eq:loss}  (5)
Rank selection criterion. Our channel pruning approach
seeks to minimize an objective function grounded in the
Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle [11, 43]
  \min _{H} \left \{ L(H) + L(D \mid H) \right \},  (6)
Here, L(D | H) quantifies the data reconstruction error,
given a model H, while L(H) represents the model’s com-
plexity or description cost. This framework naturally relates
to optimizing our Tri-NMF-like model (Y ≈ ASE). In this
analogy, the model H encompasses the factors {A, S, E}.

tations. We further penalize model size with an additional
cost proportional to rank r, scaled by a factor λ3. This com-
plete objective function therefore represents the total MDL
cost, L(H) + L(D | H), summing the data reconstruction
error with the model complexity (comprising both the struc-
tural cost influenced by orthogonality, λ2Lorth and the rank-
dependent size penalty λ3r). Figure 3 plots this total MDL

It reveals a characteristic curve that descends, as pruning
reduces the rank while the model adapts its features to bet-
ter reconstruct the data D, before reaching a global min-
imum. Beyond this point, the function starts to increase,
indicating the onset of overfitting, with the model forced to
merge components identified as most similar. This empiri-
cal minimum represents an optimal MDL trade-off; it bal-
ances compression with data fidelity, identifying the most
relevant component set, and serves as our rank selection cri-
terion. Ablation comparing reconstruction loss alone versus
total MDL cost is shown in the Supplementary Material.
4. Experiments
We conducted our main experimental evaluation on 51 dis-
tinct MS input cubes from MSTEx-1, MSTEx-2, and MS-
Bin collections, as well as from the Urban HS dataset.
MSTEx 1 & 2 [14] These collections include 20 MS cubes
of historical manuscripts from the 17th to 20th centuries,
provided by BAnQ (Bibliothèque et Archives nationales du
Quebec). Each cube consists of 8 spectral bands captured
in the range from 340nm to 1100nm. These images were
acquired using a Chroma X3 KAF 6303E (Kodak) camera
at a 6-megapixel resolution (9 µm pixel size). Ground-truth
(GT) classifies pixels into text or non-text categories only.
MSBin [17] This collection contains 30 “test” MS cubes
captured using a Phase One IQ260 achromatic camera with
a resolution of 60 megapixels, across twelve narrow-band
ranging from 365nm to 940nm. The images were digi-
tized from two manuscripts: the Bitola-Triodion ABAN 38
(Book BT) and Enina-Apostolus NBMK 1144 (Book EA),
the latter in severely degraded condition. The ground truth
identifies pixels as text foreground (iron gall ink), back-
ground, or uncertain regions, too complex for manual anno-
tations (the latter not included in evaluation). Some images
also feature a second red ink foreground, as seen on Fig. 1.
Urban [19] This dataset is a cornerstone in hyperspectral
unmixing research, which features 307×307 pixels with 162
spectral bands. Acquired by the HYDICE sensor over the
Washington DC Mall, it provides three GT scenarios with
4, 5, and 6 materials, ideal for benchmarking our rank se-
lection algorithm. With a 2×2 m² spatial resolution and a
spectral range of 400–2500 nm, it supports detailed material
identification in urban scenes with highly mixed sources.
4.1. Text extraction analysis
4.1.1. Compared methods
Given that GT exists only for text components, we employ
text binarization benchmarks to assess decomposition qual-
ity. As a baseline, we use Howe’s method [18], which is
one of the SoTA document image binarization algorithms
in the literature. We also compare the performance of five
traditional models designed for text extraction: Spatial Ker-
nel K-Harmonic Means (SKKHM) [26], Gaussian Mixture
Models (GMMs) [16], Selectional AE (SAE) [4], and Adap-
tive Coherence Estimator (ACE) v1 & v2 [7, 15]. We also
compare against two NMF models for BID, namely, MA-
ONMF, a bi-orthogonal NMF [41], and VBONMF, a Vari-
7590
ational Bayesian Orthogonal NMF [42]. Additionally, we
consider cross-domain methods, with several LMM-based
AE for unmixing: Endnet [34], MTAEU [36], DAEU [35],
OSPAEU [9], and CNNAEU [37]. We also evaluated MS-
Former [20], a promising adaptive-rank ViT-based model.
4.1.2. Metrics
To objectively evaluate the performance of the proposed
model and quantitatively compare it with state-of-the-art
methods, we employed four standard evaluation metrics:
F-Measure (FM), Distance Reciprocal Distortion (DRD),
Negative Rate Metric (NRM), and Peak Signal-to-Noise
Ratio (PSNR). FM and PSNR values are expressed as per-
centages (%) and decibels (dB), respectively, with higher
values indicating better binarization quality. Conversely,
DRD and NRM values range from 0 to 1, with lower val-
ues indicating superior binarization performance. Detailed
metric formulas are provided in Supplementary Material.
4.1.3. Text extraction results
Experimental results on the 20 cubes from MSTEx-1 and
MSTEx-2, seen in Tab. 1, confirm our model’s efficacy,
achieving the best performance across most metrics. This
underscores its strong image decomposition capabilities.
While MA-ONMF, VBONMF, and ACE2 also demon-
strated competitive results, their average performance was
lower than ours. Traditional methods such as SKKHM
and SAE yielded limited performance, in some cases even

4.2. Qualitative analysis
4.2.1. Text component
For a qualitative assessment, Figs. 1 and 4 compares our
decomposition and text extraction with different methods.
This subjective analysis is essential for capturing nuances,
like fine detail preservation, stroke continuity, and charac-
ter clarity, that quantitative metrics may not fully reflect. In
Fig. 4, most methods, including VBONMF, struggle to dis-
tinguish the overlapping stamp from handwritten text, ex-
cept for OSPAEU, MA-ONMF, and our model, confirming
PRISM’s effectiveness. On this specific sample, GMM also
demonstrates a performant text extraction, with the excep-
tion of a small stamp part, resulting in a lower FM score.

Following the similarity with the HS unmixing task, gen-
eralization of PRISM was tested on the Urban dataset. Our
model successfully extracted the abundances corresponding
to three given GT scenarios for 6, 5, and 4 materials, respec-

From twelve initial maps, (12−r) abundances are generated
after r pruning iterations, iteratively producing the results
seen in Fig. 5. To our knowledge, this is the first approach
trying to obtain the results for the three different scenarios
in a single end-to-end training (more results in Supp. Mat.).
5. Discussion
This work demonstrates that similarity-driven pruning can
serve as an effective rank-selection mechanism inside an
NMF-constrained autoencoder.
On two challenging historical-manuscript datasets with di-
verse temporal and geographical origins, PRISM consis-
tently outperforms classical and transformer-based meth-
ods, while producing interpretable abundance/endmember
pairs. The empirical success across domains suggests
that linear-mixing-based priors, when combined with
lightweight deep models, remain highly competitive for
data-scarce decomposition problems. The proposed ap-
proach offers significant potential for applications requir-
ing interpretable decomposition with minimal supervision,
such as cultural preservation or environmental monitoring.
Nevertheless, the MDL trade-off between compactness and
semantic completeness is sensitive to the pruning schedule;
aggressive compression may discard rare but meaningful
components. Future versions should therefore integrate do-
main cues (e.g., either weak labels or physics-based priors)
to prevent such failures, especially for sensitive domains.
Future work will explore semi-supervised versions to
incorporate domain knowledge, investigate reinforcement
learning for more adaptive pruning policies, and extend
the framework to natural RGB images, using PRISM as a
lightweight segmentation head on top of frozen pre-trained
encoders. In this context, the feature maps extracted by
these encoders can be interpreted as multiband images,
making our approach directly applicable for feature-space
unmixing. Finally, developing robust metrics for the seman-
tic quality of unsupervised decompositions would further
solidify the performance comparison between such models.
